Most people would bet against two of 23 people sharing a birthday, and they lose that bet about half the time. In this video, Tanmay Kulkarni shows why, using simulation and the exact calculation. He tests three things that could change the odds: how many people are in the group, how uneven the birthdays are, and how many days the birthdays can land on.

## Count pairs, not people

The key move is to stop counting people and count pairs. 23 people make 253 pairs, and each pair is a small chance. Those 253 small chances pile up faster than intuition expects. The video simulates 400,000 random groups of 23 and finds a shared birthday in 50.7% of them, and the exact calculation agrees.

## What happens as the group grows

Adding people raises the odds as you would expect: about 12% with 10 people, 71% with 30, 97% with 50, and past 99% by 57. The video also stresses what the claim does not say. It says some pair shares a birthday, not a pair that includes you. For someone to share your birthday at even odds, you need 253 other people, and with 22 others in the room your chance is under 6%.

## Uneven birthdays barely matter

So far every day was treated as equally likely, which is false. The video uses federal counts of births per day from the National Center for Health Statistics and the Social Security Administration, covering 2000 to 2014. The busiest day runs about 12% above average, and February 29th runs at about a quarter of a normal day. Rerunning the simulation on the real calendar gives 50.8% against 50.7% for even days. Uneven days can only raise the odds, and here by a tenth of a point at most. About 96% of days sit within 10% of average, so the wrong assumption was forgiving.

## The number of days decides almost everything

Squeezing the number of available days changes the answer sharply. For 23 people born inside the same 90 days, the odds are 95.4%. The video slides the window: a full year gives 50.8%, half a year 77%, 90 days 95%, and 60 days 99%. Within the same month the odds are better than 99.9%, and with a window of 7 days, 23 people cannot avoid sharing. Asked the other way, the people needed for even odds are 23 for a year, 12 for 90 days, 10 for 60 days, 7 for 30 days, 5 for a fortnight and 4 for a week.

## A square-root rule

These head counts follow a rule: take the square root of the number of days, multiply by 1.2, add a half and round. It matches every window in the film and is never more than one person off from two slots up to 2,000. For birth months, with 12 slots, it gives 5 people; four give 43% and five give 62%. The video also answers the objection that classmates are the same age: a school year with a cutoff date still contains every day of the calendar once, so it behaves like the full year, about 50.8%.

## Key takeaways

- Count pairs: 23 people make 253 pairs, which is why the odds reach 50.7%.
- The claim is about some pair matching, not a match with you.
- Group size moves the odds as expected; 10 people give about 12% and 57 give past 99%.
- Real, uneven birth data changes the result by a tenth of a point at most.
- The number of available days is the factor that matters most.
- Rule of thumb: square root of the slots, times 1.2, plus a half, rounded.
- The slots set the scale and their square root sets the crowd.

## Try it yourself

Try the video's own exercise. Everyone in a class picks a random number from 1 to 50, so how many people before two probably match? Use the rule before looking: the square root of 50 is about 7, times 1.2 plus a half, rounds to 9. Nine people give 53% and eight give 45%, well short of the intuitive guess of 25.
