The previous video in this series ended on an uncomfortable fact: an agent that got stuck can look identical to one that succeeded, right up until someone checks. Divij Pawar offers three mechanisms that turn "the agent says it worked" into something you can stand behind, and is clear about where they stop working.

## An explanation is not a process log

The starting point is that when an agent explains its reasoning, for instance "I checked the numbers, cross-referenced the filing, and concluded X," the explanation was generated by the same model that generated the conclusion. It is not a transcript of what happened but a plausible-sounding narration produced after the fact. A model that hallucinated a number can still write a coherent paragraph about how it derived it. Fluency and accuracy are different properties.

The useful question is whether any part of the explanation can be checked against something outside the model. The log is evidence of output, not of process.

## Mechanism one: parse the prose into four kinds of claims

The first move is to stop treating the reasoning as one continuous story and treat it as a pile of individual checkable assertions. A parser pulls out four types:

- Citations, wherever the text points to a source.
- Quantitative claims: dollar figures, percentages, multiples, basis points.
- Hedge language, such as "approximately" or "assumed," which flags the model's own uncertainty.
- Causal claims, sentences using "because" or "resulting in."

A line such as "revenue grew 34% year over year, driven by international expansion, source the 10-K" is tagged three ways at once: quantitative, causal and citation. None of this requires understanding the text. It is pattern matching with regular expressions, not a second AI judging the reasoning; prose is mechanically turned into a checklist of falsifiable pieces.

## Mechanism two: check citations against the real source

Extraction yields claims but does not say whether they are true. For that, the system leaves the model's head and fetches the cited source. If it is a government filing, it parses the structured data directly; otherwise it searches the raw text for numbers.

Then it checks whether a matching number appears in the source, within about 1 percent to allow for rounding. Each citation lands in one of three states: the source is reachable and a number matches; the source is reachable and nothing matches, which is a real red flag; or the fetch fails, so no judgment is possible either way. Rolled up, this gives the fraction of citations confirmed, for example six of nine, or 67 percent, which a human reviewer can act on regardless of how convincing the writing sounds.

The video is candid about a gap: the check asks whether any number in the whole reasoning trace appears in the source, so a citation can be marked confirmed on the strength of an unrelated number.

## Mechanism three: make the system disagree with itself

Much reasoning cites nothing external; it is synthesis, so there is no source to fetch. Instead, run the identical query through the full reasoning process a second time, independently, and compare the conclusions: how much the words overlap and, weighted more heavily, how much the specific numbers overlap. A model reasoning from real evidence should land in roughly the same place twice, while one confabulating a story tends to drift, especially on numbers.

The result is scored as high, medium or low agreement, and a hard flag is raised whenever a number appears in one run but not the other. If the second run says 41% and 34% appears nowhere, that is a flag, since a number present in only one of two similar runs is one of the strongest signals that it was invented rather than retrieved.

## What these checks cannot do

The video says this part matters most. None of the three mechanisms proves the agent reasoned correctly. High consistency is weak positive evidence, because two runs can agree while both are identically wrong. A citation check only confirms a number exists somewhere in the source, not that the agent's causal story about it is right. What the tools do well is catch disagreement, absence and drift: low consistency is strong negative evidence, since it is very hard to look inconsistent while being right. They reliably show when something is wrong but cannot certify that something is right.

## Key takeaways

- An agent's explanation is a post-hoc narration, not a record of its process.
- Break reasoning into four checkable claim types: citations, quantities, hedges and causes.
- Check what can be checked against outside sources, and log honestly when something cannot be checked.
- When nothing external exists, run the same query twice and compare the specific numbers.
- A number that appears in only one of two runs is a strong sign of invention.
- These methods give strong negative evidence but only weak positive evidence, so they narrow what is plausible without proving truth.

## Try it yourself

Take a real reasoning trace from your own agent, or a sample one, and tag it by hand: mark every citation, number, hedge word and causal claim. Then check one citation against the real source. If there is no external source, run the same prompt twice and see whether the specific number survives the second pass.
