AI has quietly arrived inside project tools such as Jira, Trello, Asana and Notion. It can write tickets, summarize a stand-up, and suggest what to do next. Sanjana, a project manager at Humanitarians AI, argues that the real question is not whether it is smart but which project work you should hand to it and which you should keep. The video answers with a simple three-question test.

## Autopilot or fast intern

The central idea is that the AI in these tools will not run the project for you. What it is good at is busy work: turning notes into tickets, summarizing, and tidying. The judgment stays with you.

The video describes a familiar pitch, that an AI button will basically manage your project. Lean on it that way and it will confidently commit dates it has no way to know, working from a board that is half out of date. It does not fail loudly. It fails politely, with a clean-looking answer. Treated as an autopilot it is a liability; treated as a very fast intern it is a gift.

## The three questions

Before handing anything to the AI, the video suggests asking:

1. Is it mechanical? Is the task reshaping information that already exists (summarizing, drafting, sorting), or is it a call such as choosing what matters?
2. Is it reversible? Can you check it in seconds and undo it, or do teammates act on it the moment it is posted?
3. Is the truth actually in the tool, or does it live in someone's head, a client call, or a Slack thread the AI cannot see?

Three greens means let the AI draft it. Any red means the AI can suggest, but a human decides.

## A task that passes: stand-up notes into tickets

In the first worked example, a messy stand-up note is handed to the AI with the instruction to turn it into clean tickets, decide nothing, and set no dates. Four messy lines come back as four clean cards: two bugs, a content task, and an ops note, each with a type and a rough size. Scored against the test, it is mechanical, fully reversible because all four cards can be read and fixed in about ten seconds, and everything it used was in the note. Three greens, and ten boring minutes saved.

## A task that fails: committing the sprint

The same tool and the same AI are then asked to pick the sprint and commit the dates. This scores three reds. Choosing what matters is a judgment call, the moment dates are posted five people plan around them, and the reason for the real priority was a customer call that never made it onto any card. The video adds a subtle case: even a request to summarize progress turns red if the board is stale, because the AI will then report fiction with confidence.

## The four-verb rule

The video turns this into a rule that works in any tool. Give the AI the two verbs it is built for: draft and surface. Let it draft tickets, summaries and first passes, and let it surface what is stale, unowned or blocked. Keep the two verbs that need a person: decide and commit. What the team does this sprint, what date it promises, and how someone is doing are human calls. The skill is not using AI or avoiding it, but knowing where the line is.

## Key takeaways

- Treat built-in project-tool AI as a fast intern, not an autopilot.
- AI is strong at busy work: notes to tickets, summaries, tidying.
- Test any task: is it mechanical, is it reversible, and is the truth in the tool?
- Three greens means the AI can draft it; any red means a human decides.
- AI should draft and surface; people should decide and commit.
- A stale board turns even a simple summary into confident fiction.
- A good AI answer returns clean drafts plus a short list of questions it cannot answer itself.

## Try it yourself

Open your own board and paste in a real mess, such as loose notes, half-written cards, or a stand-up transcript. Ask Claude to draft the tickets and surface what is stale, but to stop at judgment: it should not pick the sprint or commit dates, and for every call it cannot make it must ask you the one question it would need answered. If it quietly makes the decisions for you, you have found exactly what to catch.
