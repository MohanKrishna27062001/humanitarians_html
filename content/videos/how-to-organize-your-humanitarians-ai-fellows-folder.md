A reviewer sees your folder before they hear any explanation from you. This video, narrated by Liam in for Bear, shows how to organize a Humanitarians AI fellows folder so that someone else can follow your contribution, your reasoning and your actual work. The core advice is to keep the first-name, last-initial folder name, open with a clear README, give every piece of work a descriptive folder with its own report and frictional log, and make the evidence easy to open.

## Why the reviewer's view matters

Vague names and scattered logs make real work difficult to evaluate. The video notes that Professor Bear is busy and will not hunt for evidence, so the fellow's job is to organize the record. The question to ask is not how to tidy the folder in the abstract. It is how the folder lets a reviewer verify the work.

The video uses a report, a video plan and a reflection sitting in folders called "final," "new" and "stuff." Nothing in those names says which one explains the fellow's latest result.

## Start with the person

The repository names a fellow's folder with first name and last initial, all lowercase, joined by hyphens, a style the video calls kebab case. The example fellow is fictional, with a demonstration folder named example-f. You keep your existing identifier, and when names collide the repository permits an extra surname letter. A public path does not need your full name or private documents.

## Name work folders by date and topic

A dated progress folder tells a reviewer something specific: the week's report. Writing the date as year, month, day keeps folders sortable, and adding the work type and a specific topic lets a reviewer predict what is inside before opening it.

## Keep each log beside its work

Putting every reflection in one giant log fails because the reader has to match dates and guess which entry explains which piece of work. Instead, keep the frictional log beside the artifact it describes. The fellow's README can link to it without duplicating or moving the record.

## Make the README the entrance

The README states your role, project, group, research question, completed results and next steps. Beneath it, descriptive work folders branch to their own reports and evidence. A real log says what you tried and expected, where it resisted and what you did next. It names any AI or human help and what you accepted, changed or rejected, and ends with what you learned and what remains unclear. Entries are dated as you work, and late entries are labeled honestly.

Done this way, the same README leads straight to completed work, individual weekly hours and the Markdown renewal request. The example shows copied source sheets and missing finished videos, and those limits stay visible. Real fellows replace the fiction with actual records.

## What the video does not claim

This is not a mandatory or official Humanitarians AI folder template. It demonstrates one organizing pattern using a fictional example fellow. Following the structure does not guarantee renewal approval, because the underlying work and its evidence still have to exist. Claude also cannot confirm that a link works for a human reviewer, so that check belongs to the fellow.

## Key takeaways

- Keep the existing first-name, last-initial folder name in lowercase with hyphens.
- Open with a README covering role, project, group, research question, results and next steps.
- Name work folders by date, work type and topic, using year-month-day so they sort.
- Give every piece of work its own report and its own frictional log, kept beside it.
- A real log records attempts, resistance, next steps, AI or human help and what was learned.
- Date entries as you work and label late entries honestly.
- Make evidence easy to open, and keep gaps visible rather than hiding them.
