The video begins with a sentence from a science video about cancer immunotherapy: pembrolizumab produces five-year survival above 50% in metastatic melanoma. It sounds checked, and it was. A fact check caught the error and wrote a correction of about 43%, which was also wrong. Tanmay Kulkarni uses this to show how reading the cell next door in a table produces errors that spread, and how the same habit hides what actually makes immunotherapy work or fail.

## The table and the adjacent cell

The numbers come from KEYNOTE-006, in which 834 patients with advanced melanoma were randomized to pembrolizumab or ipilimumab. Five-year survival for everyone in the trial was 38.7%. The correction's 43% is a real number, 43.2%, but it belongs to the subgroup with no previous treatment, one column across. The video adds that 38.7 months is also the median survival for first-line patients, so a proportion and a length of time sit a few lines apart with the same digits. Nobody made anything up. The over-claim also bought nothing, because the ten-year results published in 2024 show 34% of the pembrolizumab group alive against 23.6% on ipilimumab.

## A correlation of 0.74

The same move appears with a statistic the field quotes widely: a 2017 letter plotting response rate against mutation count across 27 cancer types, with a correlation of 0.74. Squared, that is 0.55, so mutation count explains 55% of the difference between cancer types and leaves 45% unexplained. The video checked where that 45% lives. The formula predicts pancreatic cancer's failure within half a percentage point, but for microsatellite stable colorectal cancer it predicts about 9% responding and zero of 18 patients did. In Merkel cell carcinoma, virus-driven tumors carry fewer mutations yet responded better, which the video presents as an illustration, not a result.

## Statistical bar versus clinical bar

A randomized trial of high-dose radiation to one tumor site followed by immunotherapy, in 76 lung cancer patients, raised the response rate from 18% to 36%, often described as a doubling. The trial had set two bars in advance, a response rate of 50% and a p value under 0.1. It reached 36% with a p value of 0.07, so it cleared the statistical bar and missed the response bar.

## Hot, cold and excluded tumors

Immunotherapy takes a brake off a T cell that is already attacking, the last of six links in a chain that must happen in order. A hot tumor has T cells inside, a cold one has none nearby, and an excluded one has T cells stuck at the edge. Two trials failed at different links. A STING-activating drug injected into tumors in 47 patients produced one confirmed partial response, with immune signaling and T cells up in the blood but no significant change in infiltration into the tumor. A CXCR4 blocker plus pembrolizumab in pancreatic cancer, with 29 evaluable patients, brought CD8 T cells into the tumor as predicted, yet the response rate was 3.4%, one patient. The first moved priming but not trafficking, breaking at link four. The second moved trafficking but not killing, breaking at link six. Two broken links, one identical result.

## Where the speaker corrects himself

The video turns the lens on itself. Correcting for small samples, the honest figure for unexplained variance is closer to 40%, not 45%. In the radiation trial, patients with PD-L1 negative tumors benefited most, a half of the paper that supported his argument and which he had left out. And calling pancreatic cancer "excluded" is itself a category error, since profiling shows at least two distinct types.

## Key takeaways

- A statistic can be real and still belong to the cell next door.
- KEYNOTE-006 five-year survival was 38.7% for all patients and 43.2% for the untreated subgroup.
- A correlation of 0.74 explains about 55% of variance, leaving 45% unexplained.
- A trial can pass its statistical bar and miss its clinical bar.
- Hot, cold and excluded describe a sample, not a cancer type.
- "Cold tumor" is a symptom with at least two causes, so a fix for one does not fix the other.
- Mechanism moving and outcome moving are different findings.

## Try it yourself

Take any immunotherapy press release, abstract or conference slide and ask two questions. Which link in the immune chain did this target, and did that link move? Then check whether there is a measurement in human tissue, not a mouse, and whether the next link moved. A report passes only if it names the link and points at the measurement; the script behind every number in the video is in its description.
