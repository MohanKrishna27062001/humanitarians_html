Harshita Nimbal, an AI product designer, looks at how artificial intelligence actually changed the way people use software. She sets the hype aside and focuses on the interface itself. For 40 years, she says, UX meant one screen, built once and shown to everyone. Then the screen started guessing. The video tells that story in three acts.

## Act one: the fixed interface

The first act covers the era before AI, roughly 1984 to 2010, when every interface was fixed. The graphical interface matured, with icons, windows, menus and skeuomorphic buttons that looked like real switches. Every design decision was baked in at ship time, tested once, and shipped to millions unchanged. The same screen and menus went to a teenager and a surgeon alike, and the software had no idea who was using it.

Personalization existed, but a human had to notice a pattern and hard-code the exception. Design at that scale meant Nielsen's heuristics, style guides and A/B tests read by hand. Every rule the interface followed, a person had written down first, so the system could not learn and could only be told.

## Act two: prediction and generation

In the second act the interface stopped waiting to be told and started predicting. One app and one code base now offered a thousand front doors. A recommendation model watches what you click, and the layout itself forks, so your home page is no longer identical to your neighbor's. Prediction arrived one keystroke at a time, through autocomplete, then predictive text, then a search box finishing your sentence. By the 2010s the guess became the whole homepage, and Netflix has hundreds of millions of front pages, each assembled live from what you tend to watch.

Generative interfaces go a step further. A model reads the request and assembles the components, copy and even the layout tree, generated anew for that ask. Vercel's v0 is one example, where a typed sentence produces a working interface that no human designed. It is a loop rather than a snapshot: your clicks retrain the model, the model reshapes the interface, and your next click retrains it again. The designer's job shifts from drawing screens to shaping what the loop is allowed to learn.

## Act three: the disappearing screen

The logical endpoint is a system that no longer needs a screen. Menus collapse into a search field, and the search field collapses into a chat box, so every button becomes a sentence you type. Instead of navigating to a setting, you describe the outcome and an agent finds or builds the path. The video shows a real Claude screenshot as an example, where the sidebar holds history and files but the work happens in one box.

Beyond the chat box, the video describes an agentic interface as a workspace rather than a chat log, one where you watch the agent plan, call tools and check its own work, one visible step at a time. Its job is to narrate the machine's reasoning, not hide it.

## The cockpit exception

The speaker also names what would make the argument wrong: a domain where a fixed, hand-built interface is better. An airplane cockpit is one. Every switch is identical every time for every pilot, on purpose. Predictability is the feature there. Flexibility wins everywhere else, but certainty is what is worth optimizing for in a cockpit.

## Key takeaways

- For about 40 years, UX meant a single fixed screen built once for everyone.
- Fixed interfaces followed rules that people had written down in advance.
- Prediction let the layout fork per person, as with Netflix's homepage.
- Generative interfaces assemble the screen on request, as v0 does.
- Agentic interfaces collapse menus into conversation and should show the agent's steps.
- Control moves from the designer's hand to the model's judgment.
- Airplane cockpits remain a case where fixed interfaces are better.
