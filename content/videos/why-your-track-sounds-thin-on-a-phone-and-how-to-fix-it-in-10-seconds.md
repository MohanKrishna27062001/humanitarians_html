A track can sound full and wide in headphones and then come back thin and hollow when a friend plays it on a phone. The video, presented by Rohan Vijayakumar, a fellow at Humanitarians AI, says nothing is broken. Part of the music is canceling itself out, and it is completely predictable once you can see it.

## Why stereo needs to be folded down

Recorded music has two sides, a left and a right, slightly different from each other, and that difference is what makes music feel wide. Headphones keep the sides apart, with the left side going to your left ear and the right side to your right. A phone, a laptop or a smart speaker is a single speaker, and one speaker cannot play two things at once. It has to add the two sides together first.

## The push-and-pull door

The video explains what happens next with two people pushing the same door. If both push the same way, the door swings open easily. If one pushes while the other pulls just as hard, the door does not move at all, even though both are working. Adding two sides together does exactly this: where they agree, you get sound, and where they are opposite, you get nothing. This effect is called phase cancellation.

## What survives and what disappears

To test it, the presenter built a track and measured it. The bass and the vocal, both sitting in the center, came through untouched, identical before and after. A wide, airy layer spread across the two sides dropped by almost 50 decibels, which makes it effectively gone. What survives one speaker is whatever sits in the middle.

## Why the result is usually a hollow sound

The video notes that the result is rarely that dramatic. Most stereo width comes from delaying one side by a fraction of a millisecond. When the two sides are added together, only certain frequencies cancel. You do not lose the whole layer, you lose holes out of it, which is why a folded-down mix usually sounds hollow rather than silent.

## The 10-second mono check

The fix costs ten seconds. Before you export, switch your track to mono and listen once. If the vocal jumps forward and the air disappears, you have found the problem. The video names the usual culprits as stereo wideners, doubled parts, and wide reverb on something that should be sitting in the center. Since most people will hear your track on one speaker, it should be checked there before you call it finished.

## Try it yourself

Open your current mix, switch the master to mono and listen once. If the vocal jumps forward and the airy parts vanish, look first at any stereo widener, doubled part or wide reverb on a center element. Adjust it, then play the mix on a phone speaker to confirm that it holds together.
